# Rooted | What better conversations can change

**VC pitch research brief · November 9, 2026 · Chicago Booth New Venture Strategy**

**Investment thesis.** Can a small piece of guidance reach a parent in time to change a conversation at home? A four-week randomized text-message trial makes that plausible. Parents of 10–15-year-olds who received daily guidance subsequently rated their communication more favorably than parents receiving usual care. A separate randomized trial gives us the child’s perspective: after their parents received coaching on a specific topic, teens reported talking about that topic more often four months later. Rooted brings those two ideas together. Its distinctive bet is that knowing about *this week’s tryout* makes a question worth using when a generic tip would go unread. I found no direct test of that comparison in sixth-grade families. [MyTeen mobile trial](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2749564) · [Ford et al., topic-specific trial](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2748052)

## The studies closest to Rooted’s MVP

### 1. Can four weeks of guidance on a phone change communication at home?

**MyTeen, New Zealand.** Researchers randomized 221 caregivers of children ages 10–15 to usual care or one text a day for four weeks. Messages covered relationships, adolescent autonomy, family functioning, parent self-care and mental-health resources. The team analyzed families according to their original assignment. Of the 221 caregivers, 201 completed the three-month assessment and 214 were women. The primary outcome was the parent’s sense of competence. At one month, the adjusted advantage on that scale was 3.33 points (95% confidence interval 1.37–5.29). [MyTeen, methods and results](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2749564)

**What matters for Rooted.** Communication was a secondary outcome rated by the parent. The adjusted advantage was 2.13 scale points at one month (95% CI 0.64–3.62) and 2.21 at three months (0.48–3.95). The study shows that a lightweight mobile program can change how parents rate communication. It leaves open which messages parents used, whether children noticed a difference, and how much a two-point difference matters in daily life. The 91% follow-up rate counts completed research assessments. It would be misleading to use it as app retention. [MyTeen, outcome table](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2749564)

### 2. If a parent gets help with one topic, does the teen notice?

**Ford and colleagues, U.S. pediatric primary care.** Researchers assigned 118 parent–teen pairs to sexual-health guidance (38), alcohol-prevention guidance (40), or a control group (40). The intervention took a median of ten minutes of coaching in the clinic, clinician endorsement and a follow-up call at two weeks. At four months, 99 teens and 104 parents completed follow-up. Teens rated how frequently they had talked about each topic on a four-point scale. [Trial methods, intervention and Table 2](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2748052)

| Teen-reported conversation frequency at four months | Coached on that topic | Control | Difference on the four-point scale |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Alcohol | 2.93 | 2.40 | +0.53, p = .03 |
| Sexual health | 2.32 | 1.79 | +0.53, p = .02 |

These are unadjusted means on an ordinal scale, where one meant never or not at all and four meant often or a lot. After adjustment, the alcohol comparison remained significant (p = .03) and the sexual-health comparison was borderline (p = .05). Neither intervention shifted parents’ reports of general communication quality. The result supports a narrow prediction: if Rooted helps a parent bring up a tryout, the first thing to look for is whether that tryout comes up. Broader trust is a separate outcome. [Ford et al., Table 2 and results](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2748052)

### 3. Does the parent’s response change what the child shares next?

**Disla and colleagues observed actual exchanges.** Researchers coded adolescent disclosures and mothers’ responses moment by moment during a problem-solving discussion. They measured the time until the adolescent next shared something. Of the emotional responses they coded, expressions of interest and validation predicted the shortest interval before the next disclosure, even after accounting for age, gender and how much the participants spoke. Because the researchers observed naturally occurring responses, a more talkative or receptive pair could contribute to the pattern. [Disla et al., Social Development](https://doi.org/10.1111/SODE.12360)

This fills a gap in the listening experiment discussed below: it captures the next thing a teen actually does. The experimental study offers a different advantage because the researchers controlled the parent’s listening behavior. A product test can use both insights. After “Who was easy to work with at practice today?”, suggest a response that follows the child’s answer, such as “What did she do that helped?” Let the parent mark whether there was an opportunity for a follow-up. Allow “wrong moment” as a response so a child choosing privacy does not become a failed conversation. [Observed exchanges](https://doi.org/10.1111/SODE.12360) · [Randomized listening experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34087604/)

## Why parents may need an opening

**[Research] Parents sometimes hesitate to start conversations their children value.** Gallup and the Walton Family Foundation surveyed 1,675 parent or guardian and child pairs online in March 2024. Among parents worried about social media, 61% of those comfortable discussing it said they brought it up often, compared with 33% of those less comfortable. Across all children surveyed, 83% found it helpful to talk with a parent about their social media experiences. The sample covered ages 10–18. These are self-reports across that age range, and the 83% comes from a broader group than the two parent percentages. [Gallup survey and comparison groups](https://news.gallup.com/poll/645602/parents-avoid-hard-helpful-conversations-gen.aspx)

**[Team / pilot implication]** Test a question about a specific moment the parent might struggle to bring up. Give the parent an option to listen before offering advice. When Gallup asked what kids wanted a parent to do when they were upset, 62% selected listening and 28% selected advice. Children could choose more than one response. [Gallup, child responses](https://news.gallup.com/poll/645602/parents-avoid-hard-helpful-conversations-gen.aspx)

**How representative is Gallup?** The parent sample came from a U.S. probability panel recruited through address-based sampling. Each participating adult had one child respond. Gallup gives a 95% margin of sampling error of ±3.3 percentage points for the full child sample. The worried-parent subgroup is smaller, so that margin cannot be used for its 61% and 33% estimates. Parent comfort could shape frequency, and repeated positive conversations could also make parents more comfortable. In an interview, ask for the last *specific* conversation a parent hesitated to start, how they opened it and what happened next. That would tell us whether an opener addresses their obstacle. [Full Gallup report and methodology](https://www.gallup.com/file/analytics/651698/Walton-Family-Foundation-Gallup-Parenting-Generation-Z-2024-Report.pdf)

**Why start at sixth grade?** Padilla-Walker and colleagues followed 500 adolescents annually from ages 12 to 18. They estimated that 82% followed a pattern of gradually sharing less about their whereabouts and activities, 13% consistently shared little, and 5% declined steeply before leveling off. The measure concerns routine disclosure. It does not establish that emotional closeness drops suddenly when a child starts middle school. The closest geographic precedent is the SUPPER trial below. Its eligibility criteria specified Massachusetts families with fifth-, sixth- or seventh-graders. Its outcomes therefore deserve more weight for Boston recruitment than results from adult strangers. Household willingness to pay was outside both studies. [Disclosure trajectories](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29283598/) · [SUPPER enrollment protocol](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8809599/) · [Completed trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40981732/)

## What changes for families?

| Outcome | What the researchers tested and found | What Rooted can claim |
|---|---|---|
| **Teens report more open communication** | In a randomized trial of 120 families with teens ages 13–15, parents received a booklet, clinician endorsement and instructions to discuss each other’s strengths. At two months, teens who started with low openness scores reported better communication than controls. The estimated difference was 3.55 scale points (95% CI 1.07–6.03). Parent-reported communication showed no effect. | A specific, reciprocal discussion can be part of an effective intervention for families already struggling to talk. The trial evaluated the whole package. The contribution of the conversation alone is unknown. [Journal of Pediatrics, 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32444221/) |
| **Families report less conflict** | Schools in Melbourne were randomly assigned to intervention or control conditions. Researchers followed 225 caregivers and 224 students from grade 6 into grade 7. A six-session program taught parents to recognize emotions, regulate their own reactions and coach their children through difficult feelings. The study found reductions in reported family conflict and acting-out difficulties. | This is evidence from Rooted’s exact school transition. It supports testing guidance on how to respond once a child opens up. Six sessions of coaching are a much larger commitment than a pickup-line tip. [Original trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26005933/) · [Program and follow-up details from the same cohort](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32077729/) |
| **Families discuss specific hard topics** | The SUPPER randomized trial analyzed 388 families. Children averaged 11.8 years old. Parents received a handbook, two sessions and text messages to encourage substance-use conversations around family meals. The comparison group received equally structured support on other topics. At three months, intervention parents reported gains on nine of ten targeted communication items. Children reported gains on three. Some effects remained at 18 months. | An ordinary family routine can be a setting for a targeted conversation program. The results also show why a parent score of 7/10 cannot tell us whether the child felt heard. [SUPPER trial, Journal of Adolescent Health](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40981732/) |
| **A change in behavior outside the conversation** | Families Talking Together enrolled 900 families with adolescents ages 11–14 in a pediatric clinic. At 12 months, teens reported more communication on sexual-health topics. In the intervention group, 4.7% reported first having intercourse during the follow-up period, compared with 14.7% in controls. | A caregiver program can affect a measurable behavior in a specific setting. This intervention also involved clinicians and parenting strategies. The trial cannot tell us how much of the effect came from conversation alone. Use this in investor Q&A if asked whether outcomes can extend beyond feeling closer. [Pediatrics, 2020, full trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7193976/) |

## How large are the downstream effects across studies?

**A pooled estimate gives the pitch a reality check.** Widman and colleagues synthesized 31 U.S. evaluations covering 12,464 adolescents with a mean age of 12.3. Fourteen independent estimates contributed to the communication outcome. Parent-based sexual-health programs improved youth-reported communication about sex by an average of 0.27 standard deviations (95% CI 0.19–0.35). That is a small average gain. It does not mean 27% more conversations. Programs also improved reported condom use (0.32 standard deviations, 95% CI 0.13–0.51). Across 20 estimates, the pooled result for delaying sexual activity was inconclusive (−0.06, 95% CI −0.14 to 0.02). Most programs were delivered in person. The later Families Talking Together trial above had a favorable result on sexual initiation, but its result should not become a general claim about what a conversation does. [Widman et al., JAMA Pediatrics, pooled outcomes and intervention characteristics](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6664375/)

**Mental health is a reason the relationship matters.** Zapf and colleagues screened 5,314 articles. Their 37 eligible reports represented 26 distinct studies, with several papers using the same participants. They generally found small-to-medium associations between adolescent-rated communication quality and mental health. Most designs were observational. A young person experiencing depression may find conversation harder, and family stress can influence both communication and symptoms. An investor can reasonably ask why closeness matters. The research supports that discussion, while a promise to improve depression, self-esteem or long-term health would require a trial measuring that outcome. [Zapf et al., systematic review, study characteristics and limitations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11143954/)

**Employer delivery has a precedent.** Talking Parents, Healthy Teens randomized 569 working parents with children in grades 6–10. The program ran for eight weekly one-hour workplace sessions. At nine months, 38% of intervention parents reported discussing seven or more new sexual-health topics, compared with 8% of controls. Adolescents also reported differences in the number of topics discussed. This is evidence that working parents recruited through employers can use communication training. Rooted would still need to test employer purchasing, enrollment without research support and repeat use of a much shorter intervention. [Schuster et al., BMJ trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18617492/)

## What makes a conversation worth returning to?

**[Research] What parents do after the first answer matters.** In a preregistered experiment, 1,001 teens ages 13–16 watched a parent listen to a teen sharing a difficult experience. Those who saw better listening reported greater willingness to tell that parent more in the future. The experiment measured intention. A separate three-wave study of 982 youths found a related pattern in family life: positive parental reactions predicted greater feelings of connection, which in turn predicted more disclosure. The second study followed families over time and cannot establish cause. Together they give Rooted a reason to put the first response tip in the MVP. [Listening experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34087604/) · [Longitudinal disclosure study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19716171/)

**[Research] The quality of the relationship matters beyond that day.** A U.S. cohort of more than 15,000 adolescents linked warmth and satisfaction with parent communication in adolescence to favorable health outcomes in young adulthood. Communication satisfaction was more consistently associated with later outcomes than the amount of communication. This is a reason to care about how conversations feel. It cannot establish a health benefit from adding a daily question. [JAMA Network Open cohort study, 2023](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2802677)

**[Research] Digital parent coaching can change what parents report.** In the Partners in Parenting randomized trial, 359 parents enrolled and 332 adolescents took part. At 12 months, the online program outperformed educational factsheets on parent-rated parenting practices, with a standardized effect of 0.51 (95% CI 0.30–0.72). Adolescents’ ratings of parenting showed no significant intervention effect. Both groups received encouragement calls. For Rooted, label parent feedback as the parent’s experience and measure repeat use separately. [Yap et al., Journal of Medical Internet Research, 2019](https://www.jmir.org/2019/8/e13628/)

## What we should take into the pilot

1. **Test one question tied to a real event.** Within each of the 30 pilot families, randomly alternate event-specific prompts and equally well-written generic prompts across comparable moments. Keep timing and effort similar. Compare the share of delivered prompts the parent confirms asking. Report unanswered check-ins separately. This tests the value of entering a schedule.
2. **Give the parent a second move.** For example, after tryouts: “What was one moment you wish I’d seen?” If the child shares, the parent can ask, “What was that like for you?” Give the child room to pass. Track whether parents find the listening tip useful before investing in deeper levels, streaks or groups.
3. **Measure what each person can report.** Track repeat use per enrolled family, week-four retention and whether the parent would use the prompt again. A 7/10 is a parent rating. A future claim that kids feel closer would require evidence from kids, with a separately designed, voluntary evaluation. The parent-only MVP can establish usefulness to parents.
4. **Keep the commercial claims open.** These studies do not test calendar-based AI, willingness to pay $9.99, retention or whether one family’s ratings improve another’s prompts. Use a real paid offer and report the number who accept out of everyone offered it. Treat the four-week pilot as an early demand test.

### What would persuade us the schedule is worth entering?

**Primary comparison.** Before week one, define an eligible moment, such as pickup on a day the family says it will be together. Randomize prompt type within each family across those moments and keep notification timing, topic sensitivity and response tips comparable. Use parent-confirmed questions asked divided by all delivered prompts. If a parent misses a check-in, classify the outcome as unknown and show how the comparison changes when unknowns are excluded. An open notification measures exposure only.

**Interpret the four weeks at the family level.** Report the average within-family difference in use between event-specific and generic prompts, the number of families favoring each type, and the number with too little feedback to compare. Thirty parents answering repeatedly still represent 30 families. A rising average conversation score can reflect the least engaged families leaving. Show week-four use among all 30 original families alongside ratings from those still responding. Fifteen active families would meet the team’s 50% target, with a roughly 33%–67% 95% Wilson interval. That is enough to decide what to test next and too imprecise for a market-wide retention claim.

**Decision rule.** If parents keep using both prompt types equally, investigate whether entering the schedule earns its cost. If personalized prompts win only among families who already log events regularly, test easier schedule input before expanding the question library. If parents use the prompts but decline the paid offer, report engagement and payment separately. These are proposed pilot decisions, not findings from the cited research.

## Slide-ready wording

**[Research]** “In a randomized trial, teens who initially found it hard to talk with their parents reported more open communication two months after their families received guidance to discuss each other’s strengths.” [Journal of Pediatrics](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32444221/)

**[Team / hypothesis]** “Rooted gives a parent one question tied to their sixth grader’s week and one way to follow up. Our first test is whether parents use those questions more often than generic ones and come back the next week.”

**Evidence scope.** This targeted research summary prioritizes original trials, experiments and longitudinal studies with outcomes relevant to the product. The brand comparison uses Hinge’s own data. None of the studies evaluated Rooted, and the six-session coaching findings come from one cohort. The research supports testing the product. The pilot will supply the first customer evidence.
